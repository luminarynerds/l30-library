A Love Letter to Our Library

From the people who show up every day

Dear L30 Library,

We never thought we'd write a love letter to a building. But you were never really a building, were you? You were the place where Denise's daughter read her first chapter book. Where Mr. Gutierrez taught himself English using your language CDs and a patience we all admired. Where half the teenagers in town pretend they're doing homework but are actually just glad to have somewhere warm that doesn't ask them to buy anything.

You smelled like old paper and possibility. You still do.

When the schools cut their library staff, you picked up the slack without being asked. When the pandemic closed everything else, you left bags of books on the front steps in the rain. When the city council said maybe we didn't need a library anymore because "everything's online now," you showed up to that meeting with 200 signatures and a presentation that made Councilwoman Davies cry.

We know it hasn't been easy. We saw the lights flicker. We noticed when the magazine section got smaller. We heard the staff talking quietly about budget cuts when they thought no one was listening. We were listening.

And now you've gone and done something extraordinary. You looked at a system that was bleeding you dry and you said: no more. Not with anger. With clarity. You took our money — the taxes we pay, the donations we give, the fines we pretend to be annoyed about — and you decided it should actually go toward serving us. What a concept.

We love that you hired a real person instead of renewing a contract. We love that you're training the next generation of librarians right here. We love that when you started using AI, the first thing you did was write a policy protecting our privacy. Not because anyone made you. Because that's who you are.

You're seven people in a building on Main Street, and you operate like you're responsible for the intellectual future of this entire community. Because you are. And you know it.

So here's our Valentine to you: We see you. We're proud of you. And we'll fight for you the way you've always fought for us.

With love, gratitude, and a few overdue books,
The People of L30
All 4,200 of us.
P.S. — We promise to return the Gutierrez family's copy of Becoming. Eventually.

It's Not You, It's Them

After years of rising costs, locked-down data, and one-size-fits-none solutions, L30 Library is charting a new course. Here's why we broke up with our vendors - and what we're building instead.

A Letter to Our Patrons

From the desk of the Library Director

Dear L30,

For years, we've smiled through vendor contracts the way you smile through a bad date, convincing ourselves it would get better. That the prices would stabilize. That we'd eventually get access to our own data. That the platform our patrons actually used would stop changing its terms every fiscal quarter.

It didn't get better.

Last year, we spent 62% of our materials budget on digital content licensing. Content we don't own, can't preserve, and could lose access to if a single corporation decides our contract isn't profitable enough. Our patrons waited an average of 14 weeks for popular ebook titles. And when we asked for our own circulation data in a usable format, we were told it would be a "custom report" at additional cost.

We were paying for the privilege of not owning anything.

So this Valentine's Day, we're making it official: we broke up with the old way of doing things. Not out of spite. Not because we think technology is the enemy. But because we believe this community deserves a library system that actually belongs to it.

We've spent the past eight months quietly rebuilding. New systems. New tools. A new approach to technology that puts our staff and patrons, not shareholders, at the center. We're using AI to help our staff spend less time on busywork and more time doing what they do best: connecting people with information, with stories, with each other.

It hasn't been easy. We've made mistakes. Our catalog looked like a crime scene for about three weeks in October. But we learned, we adapted, and we built something we're proud of. With our own hands, our own data, and our own values.

And here's the part that still makes us a little emotional: the money we're saving on vendor contracts? We're hiring with it. One new full-time position this year, with benefits, funded by the budget we clawed back from licensing fees. And our Board didn't stop there. They insisted that this position come with a salary that sets a new standard for what professional librarians should be paid. Not competitive. Not "comparable." The standard.

We're also launching a paid six-month SLIS internship, two cohorts per year. Here's the thing nobody talks about: libraries have always wanted to mentor new professionals. They just never had the time. When your staff isn't buried in data entry, vendor ticket queues, and three redundant systems, they actually have capacity to teach. Our staff asked for this. They want to train the next generation of librarians who know they don't have to accept the way things have always been done.

Can we be honest about something else? Budget season used to be miserable. And it shouldn't be. We'd sit in a room and fight each other over scraps. Do we cut programming or periodicals? Do we keep the database nobody uses or the one everybody loves but costs twice as much? Good people arguing against each other because the money was already spoken for by contracts we couldn't renegotiate. That's not how a team should work. That's not how a community cornerstone should feel.

It's still not easy. We're not pretending open-source is magic or that AI solves everything. But it's pragmatic. The conversations are different now. We're talking about what we want to build, not what we're forced to cut. We're all happier. We hope you are, too.

This is what a library looks like when it decides to stop asking permission.

With love and absolutely no regrets, The Staff of L30 Library All seven of us.

How We Got Here

A brief history of paying too much for too little

2011

The Digital Promise

We signed our first ebook lending contract. The pitch was irresistible: unlimited access, modern library, happy patrons. The fine print was less exciting.

2016

The Creeping Costs

License fees increased 40% over five years while our budget grew 3%. We started canceling print periodicals to cover digital costs. The irony was not lost on us.

2021

The Data Question

We asked for a full export of our patron usage data for a state report. We were told the data was proprietary to the platform. Our patrons' reading habits - collected in our library - weren't ours to analyze.

2025

The Breaking Point

A contract renewal arrived with a 28% price increase, reduced title availability, and new terms restricting how we could promote the service. We looked at each other across the conference table and said what we'd been thinking for years.

2026

The Rebuild

New infrastructure. Local control. AI-augmented workflows designed by our own staff. A library system that runs on community values, not vendor quarterly targets.

Our AI Policy

Adopted unanimously by the Board of Trustees, January 2026

§

Responsible AI Use Policy

L30 Library - Board Resolution 2026-003

Purpose

This policy establishes guidelines for the ethical, transparent, and community-centered use of artificial intelligence tools within the L30 Library. AI is a tool - not a replacement for the professional judgment, empathy, and community knowledge our staff brings to their work every day.

Guiding Principles

I.

Staff empowerment, not replacement. AI tools are implemented to reduce administrative burden and amplify our staff's ability to serve patrons. No AI system will be used to reduce staffing levels, monitor employee performance, or replace professional librarian judgment in collection development or reader advisory services.

II.

Patron privacy is non-negotiable. No patron data - circulation records, search histories, program attendance, or any personally identifiable information - will be used to train AI models, shared with third-party AI providers, or analyzed without explicit aggregation and anonymization. We'd rather lose a tool than lose a patron's trust.

III.

Transparency by default. When patrons interact with an AI-assisted service, they will know it. When AI informs a recommendation, we'll say so. When we're not sure if an AI output is reliable, we'll say that too. No black boxes in a public library.

IV.

Data sovereignty. All AI tools used by this library system will process data on infrastructure we control or through agreements that guarantee our data is not retained, trained on, or sold. Our community's information stays in our community.

V.

Continuous learning and honest assessment. We will regularly evaluate AI tools for bias, accuracy, and actual usefulness. If a tool isn't serving our patrons well, we'll fix it or drop it - regardless of how much time we spent implementing it. Sunk cost is not a library value.

Current Applications

AI tools are currently used at L30 Library for: automated cataloging assistance and metadata enrichment, first-draft responses to common reference inquiries (always reviewed by staff before delivery), program scheduling optimization, collection analysis and gap identification, and accessibility improvements including real-time translation support for our growing multilingual community.

What We Don't Use AI For

Patron surveillance or behavioral tracking. Content filtering or censorship decisions. Replacing human interaction at the service desk. Any application that would make our library less human, less welcoming, or less free.

This policy is a living document subject to annual review. Community input is welcome and encouraged. Last revised January 14, 2026.
