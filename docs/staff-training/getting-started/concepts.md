# Library concepts you'll mix up

## What this is for

Pairs of terms a new staffer commonly confuses, with scenarios and the right thing to say at the desk.

## When you'd use it

- In your first two weeks on the job, before muscle memory takes over.
- When prepping a patron-facing message you want to get right.

## bib vs item vs copy

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [bib](glossary.md#bib-bibliographic-record): the cataloging record for one edition.
- [item](glossary.md#item): a lendable thing attached to a bib.
- [copy](glossary.md#copy): one physical artifact, with its own barcode.

**Mental model.** Bib is the recipe. Item is the lendable thing. Copy is the artifact you can hold. Three copies of the same book share one bib and three item records. A different edition gets its own bib.

**A scenario.** A patron asks "do you have *Pride and Prejudice*?" You search the catalog and see two bibs: a 2008 Penguin Classics edition and a 1995 Modern Library edition. Branch A holds two copies of the Penguin and Branch B holds two copies of the Modern Library. The patron wanted a paperback for a flight tomorrow; the Modern Library copies are hardback. You can't answer the question with one number.

**What staff say wrong.** "We have one *Pride and Prejudice*." Or: "We have four *Pride and Prejudice*s."

**What to say instead.** "We have two editions and four copies. Two paperbacks at Branch A and two hardbacks at Branch B. Which works for you?"

## hold vs hold shelf vs hold queue vs account hold

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [hold](glossary.md#hold): the request a patron places.
- [hold shelf](glossary.md#hold-shelf): the physical shelf where ready holds wait.
- [hold queue](glossary.md#hold-queue): the order patrons are in for the same bib.
- [account hold](glossary.md#account-hold): any condition preventing normal circulation. Unrelated to a patron hold on an item.

**Mental model.** A hold is the request. The hold shelf is the place. The hold queue is the order. An account hold is unrelated terminology debt -- some libraries rename it to `account block` to avoid this exact mess. L30 keeps both names; you have to disambiguate at the desk.

**A scenario.** A patron walks up and says "is my hold ready?" You pull up their account and see two things: a request for *Babel* with `ready` status (their item is on the hold shelf), and a flag at the top of the account saying "account hold: $4.20 in overdue fines." If you say "you have a hold" they hear "my book is ready." If you say "you have an account hold" they hear "my book is ready." Neither lands.

**What staff say wrong.** "You have an account hold so we can't process your hold." A patron has no chance of parsing that.

**What to say instead.** "Your *Babel* hold is ready on the hold shelf, but there's an account block we need to clear first -- it's $4.20 in fines. Want to take care of that now?"

## fine vs fee vs lost-item charge

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [fine](glossary.md#fine): accrues over time, usually from overdue days.
- [fee](glossary.md#fee): a one-time charge, like processing.
- [lost-item charge](glossary.md#lost-item-charge): replacement plus processing when an item is declared lost.

**Mental model.** If a clock is ticking, it is a fine. If it's a one-time stamp, it is a fee. If the patron has to replace something, it's a lost-item charge. Waiver authority differs across all three at most libraries; you may be able to waive a fine but not a lost-item charge without supervisor sign-off.

**A scenario.** A patron asks "what do I owe?" The account shows three lines: $1.40 overdue (a fine, still climbing), $5.00 hold-pickup-noshow (a fee, frozen), and $24.00 for a lost board book (a lost-item charge, frozen unless the book turns up). If you read it as "$30.40 in fines" you've told the patron you can waive the lot, which you probably can't, and you've also misled them about the board book situation.

**What staff say wrong.** "You owe a $25 fine for the lost book."

**What to say instead.** "You have a $24 lost-item charge for the board book, plus $1.40 in overdue fines and a $5 no-show fee. The fines and fee I can help with at the desk; the lost-item charge needs a supervisor or the book turning up."

## suspension vs restriction vs block

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [suspension](glossary.md#suspension): automatic, time-bounded. Lifts when the date passes.
- [restriction](glossary.md#restriction): staff-applied. Staff add it, staff remove it.
- [account block](glossary.md#account-block) (and [account hold](glossary.md#account-hold)): system condition (overdue, unpaid fees, expired card) that lifts when the condition clears.

**Mental model.** Suspension: clock runs out and you're back in. Restriction: staff put it on, staff take it off. Block: fix the underlying condition (pay the fine, renew the card) and it lifts itself. Patrons need different next steps for each, so the words matter.

**A scenario.** A patron says "my card isn't working." You check and see one of three things: a suspension that ends Friday, a restriction Marcia in tech-services added two weeks ago, or a block because their card expired Monday. "Wait until Friday," "Marcia is your person on Monday," and "renew at the desk right now" are three different conversations.

**What staff say wrong.** "Your card is suspended" when it's actually blocked because their card expired.

**What to say instead.** "Your card expired Monday -- that's why it's not working. We can renew it right now and you're back in." Or: "Your account is suspended through Friday after three overdue notices. After Friday it'll work again." Or: "There's a restriction Marcia put on Monday; let me message her and we'll sort it out."

## registration vs enrollment vs sign-up

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [registration](glossary.md#registration): in L30, reading-program-specific.
- enrollment: not used in L30 (avoid).
- sign-up: for events.

**Mental model.** In L30, you `register` for reading programs, you `sign up` for events, and you don't `enroll` in anything. Patrons and outside library culture use these interchangeably; staff don't, because the three actions hit different parts of the system.

**A scenario.** Youth-services posts a flyer that says "enroll for summer reading at the front desk." A parent shows up with two kids and says "I want to enroll." If you take that at face value, you might end up in event sign-up (wrong), or trying to create new library accounts (also wrong; they might already have cards). What they want is reading-program registration, which is its own page.

**What staff say wrong.** "Let me enroll your kid."

**What to say instead.** "Let me register your kid in the summer reading program. Do they already have a library card?"

## patron vs household vs family group

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [patron](glossary.md#patron): the individual record.
- [household](glossary.md#household): patron-record-level grouping by shared address.
- [family group](glossary.md#family-group): reading-program-scoped only.

**Mental model.** A patron is one person with one card. A household lives on the patron record and ties accounts together by guardian relationship; it persists across everything. A family group is a reading-program construct, scoped to that program, and lets a parent log on behalf of their kids inside the program. A household can have a family group inside a program; a family group with no household is fine too.

**A scenario.** A parent says "I want to register the whole family for summer reading." You don't yet know whether they have a household set up. If you treat the request as a household-level action you may add or remove the wrong people. If you treat it as a family-group request you may miss someone who isn't in the household yet.

**What staff say wrong.** "You're already in a family."

**What to say instead.** "You have a household on your account already -- let's add a family group for the summer reading program, so progress shows for everyone."

## role vs function tag vs dashboard function

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [role](glossary.md#role): the staff job category, which gates pages and actions.
- [function tag](glossary.md#function-tag): the literal string value of a dashboard function.
- [dashboard function](glossary.md#dashboard-function): which dashboard panels render.

**Mental model.** Role is who you are. Dashboard function is what your home screen shows. Function tag is the string in the database that ties the two together. These are L30 idioms -- they won't transfer to another ILS, so don't use them with vendor support or external trainers. The new-staffer profile screen surfaces all three fields, which is where the confusion starts.

**A scenario.** A new staffer asks why their dashboard is empty. You look at their profile: their role is `circulation` (good, they can use the check-out page), but their dashboard function is unset (so the dashboard shows nothing). Telling them "set your function to circulation" is wrong on two counts: you mean dashboard function, and the value is `front_desk`, not `circulation`.

**What staff say wrong.** "Set your function to circulation."

**What to say instead.** "Set your role to `circulation` so you can use the check-out page, and set your dashboard function to `front_desk` so your dashboard loads the circulation panels."

## checkout vs checkin vs discharge vs in transit

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [checkout](glossary.md#checkout): item out to a patron.
- check-in (called `check in` in L30 UI): item back at its owning branch.
- [discharge](glossary.md#discharge): some ILSes' word for check-in. L30 doesn't use it.
- [in transit](glossary.md#in-transit): the item is moving between branches; don't touch it.

**Mental model.** Checkout sends it out. Check-in brings it back home. Discharge is a synonym you'll see in vendor docs and older training material; treat it as check-in. In transit is the middle state: the item left one branch, hasn't reached the other, and isn't yours to process. Most damage from new staff comes from forcing a transaction on an in-transit item.

**A scenario.** A patron drops off a book at your branch. You scan it and the screen says `in transit -- owned by Branch B`. The patron asks "am I good?" The instinct is to check it in. The right move is to leave the in-transit status alone, drop it in the transit bin, and tell the patron the system has it.

**What staff say wrong.** "Let me check this back in for you" (then forces it through, which can scramble the transit record).

**What to say instead.** "The system has it -- it's in transit back to Branch B, and I'll put it in the transit bin. You're good."

## claims returned vs claims never had

**The terms** (from [glossary.md](glossary.md)):
- [claims returned](glossary.md#claims-returned--claims-never-had): patron says they brought it back, system shows it still out.
- claims never had: patron says they never checked it out in the first place.

**Mental model.** Both are *disputes*, not statuses you clear. A dispute opens a documented review: someone walks the shelf, checks the bin, looks at the camera if there is one, and either resolves it (item turns up) or escalates it. The wrong move is to discharge the item to make the patron happy, because you've now lost the audit trail and the item is still missing.

**A scenario.** A patron at the desk says "I returned that DVD weeks ago, why is it still on my account?" You can see it was checked out six weeks ago, no check-in record. You're tempted to discharge it and move on -- the patron is frustrated, the line is growing. Don't.

**What staff say wrong.** "OK let me discharge that for you." (Or: "let me check it back in.")

**What to say instead.** "I'll flag it as claims-returned and someone will check the shelf and the bin. It'll resolve in a few days. If it doesn't turn up, we can talk about next steps then." For claims never had: "I'll flag it as claims-never-had and we'll look at the checkout record. You won't owe anything in the meantime."
